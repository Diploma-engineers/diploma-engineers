1.1 Structure of Atom

An atom is the smallest electrically neutral unit of an element that takes part in a chemical reaction and retains the chemical identity of that element. It contains a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. The nucleus contains positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. Almost all the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus, while most of its volume is occupied by the electron cloud.

Atomic number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, number of electrons is also Z.
Mass number (A): Total number of protons and neutrons. Therefore, A = Z + N, where N is the number of neutrons.

Rutherford’s nuclear model

Ernest Rutherford proposed the nuclear model after the alpha-particle scattering experiment. A beam of fast alpha particles was directed at a very thin gold foil and the scattered particles were detected on a fluorescent screen.

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, showing that most of the atom is empty space.
  • A small number were deflected through small angles, indicating repulsion from a positively charged region.
  • A very few bounced back or were deflected through large angles, showing that nearly all positive charge and mass are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus.

Conclusions: An atom has a tiny positively charged nucleus; electrons revolve around it; electrostatic attraction holds electrons near the nucleus. Limitations: Classical physics predicts that revolving electrons should continuously radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus. The model also could not explain the line spectrum of hydrogen or the arrangement of electrons.

Rutherford nuclear model of the atom.

Bohr’s theory and hydrogen spectrum

Niels Bohr improved the nuclear model by proposing that electrons can occupy only certain permitted circular orbits, called stationary states or energy levels. An electron in an allowed orbit does not continuously lose energy. Energy is absorbed when it moves to a higher level and emitted when it falls to a lower level.

  • Each permitted orbit has a definite energy.
  • Only those orbits are allowed for which the electron’s angular momentum is quantised.
  • Radiation is emitted or absorbed only during a transition between two energy levels.
Energy of photon: ΔE = Ehigher − Elower = hν = hc/λ

Here h is Planck’s constant, ν is frequency, c is speed of light and λ is wavelength. When an excited hydrogen electron falls to a lower energy level, it emits a photon of a particular wavelength. Because only certain energy differences are possible, hydrogen produces a line spectrum rather than a continuous spectrum.

Importance: Bohr’s model explains the hydrogen line spectrum and the stability of hydrogen atom. Limitations: It does not satisfactorily explain spectra of multi-electron atoms, fine spectral lines or some effects of magnetic and electric fields.

Heisenberg uncertainty principle

It is impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both the position and momentum of a microscopic particle such as an electron. The more accurately one is measured, the less accurately the other can be known.

Δx × Δp ≥ h / 4π    or    Δx × Δv ≥ h / (4πm)

This is not due merely to poor instruments; it is a fundamental property of microscopic particles. It explains why electrons are described by probability distributions (orbitals), not fixed circular paths.

1.2 Quantum Numbers and Orbitals

Quantum numbers describe the energy, shape, orientation and spin state of an electron in an atom. Four quantum numbers are used.

Quantum numberSymbolMeaning and allowed values
PrincipalnMain shell and approximate energy/size; n = 1, 2, 3…
AzimuthallSubshell and shape; l = 0 to n−1. 0=s, 1=p, 2=d, 3=f.
MagneticmlOrientation; integer values from −l to +l.
SpinmsElectron spin; +½ or −½.

An orbital is a three-dimensional region around the nucleus where the probability of finding an electron is high. One orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins.

Shapes of s, p and d orbitals

  • s orbital: Spherical; one orientation; every s subshell contains one orbital and can hold 2 electrons.
  • p orbitals: Dumbbell-shaped, with three mutually perpendicular orientations px, py and pz; total capacity 6 electrons.
  • d orbitals: Five orbitals. Four commonly have a four-lobed clover-like shape; dz² has two lobes and a doughnut-shaped ring. Total capacity 10 electrons.
Simplified visual representations; actual orbital boundaries are probability surfaces.

Rules for filling electrons

Aufbau principle: Electrons occupy orbitals in order of increasing energy. The (n + l) rule is used: the orbital with lower n+l fills first; if values are equal, the lower n fills first.

Pauli exclusion principle: No two electrons in the same atom can have all four quantum numbers identical. Therefore, an orbital holds at most two electrons and they must have opposite spins.

Hund’s rule of maximum multiplicity: In degenerate orbitals (orbitals of equal energy), electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing begins. This reduces electron-electron repulsion.

Common filling order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s…

Electronic configuration

Electronic configuration is the distribution of electrons among shells, subshells and orbitals. For example: H = 1s¹; He = 1s²; C = 1s² 2s² 2p²; O = 1s² 2s² 2p⁴; Na = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹; Cl = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵.

For many transition elements, the 4s subshell fills before 3d, but when transition-metal cations form, 4s electrons are generally removed before 3d electrons. Some configurations, such as Cr and Cu, show extra stability associated with half-filled or completely filled d subshells: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

1.3 Chemical Bonding

A chemical bond is the attractive force that holds atoms or ions together in a stable arrangement. Atoms form bonds because the bonded arrangement is usually at lower potential energy and more stable than the separated atoms. Many main-group atoms tend to attain a noble-gas-like valence-shell configuration, although this is a useful tendency rather than a universal rule.

Ionic (electrovalent) bond

An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another atom, producing oppositely charged ions that attract each other electrostatically. Sodium transfers its one valence electron to chlorine.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻     Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻     Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

NaCl forms a three-dimensional ionic crystal lattice, not separate NaCl molecules in the solid state. Ionic compounds generally have high melting and boiling points, are often soluble in polar solvents such as water, and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved because ions can move. They do not normally conduct in the solid state.

Covalent bond

A covalent bond forms when atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. It commonly occurs between non-metal atoms. A single bond contains one shared pair; a double bond contains two shared pairs; a triple bond contains three shared pairs.

  • H₂: Each hydrogen contributes one electron; one shared pair gives H—H.
  • F₂: Each fluorine shares one electron and retains three lone pairs, giving F—F.
  • HF: Hydrogen and fluorine share one pair. The bond is polar because fluorine attracts the shared pair more strongly.

Electronegativity and polarity: When bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the shared electron pair is displaced toward the more electronegative atom. This creates partial charges (δ+ and δ−) and a polar covalent bond.

Hybridisation and molecular shapes

Hybridisation is the mixing of atomic orbitals of comparable energy on the same atom to form a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals oriented in space. It helps explain molecular geometry and bond direction.

BeCl₂: Beryllium forms two sp hybrid orbitals directed 180° apart. Each overlaps with a chlorine orbital to form a sigma bond; the molecule is linear in the gaseous state.

BF₃: Boron forms three sp² hybrid orbitals in one plane at 120°. Each forms a B—F sigma bond, giving a trigonal planar molecule.

CH₄: Carbon forms four equivalent sp³ hybrid orbitals directed toward the corners of a tetrahedron. Each overlaps with a hydrogen 1s orbital to form a C—H sigma bond.

NH₃: Nitrogen has three bond pairs and one lone pair in an approximately sp³ arrangement. The lone pair repels bond pairs more strongly, so the shape is trigonal pyramidal and the angle is about 107°.

H₂O: Oxygen has two bond pairs and two lone pairs. Stronger lone-pair repulsions compress the H—O—H angle to about 104.5°, producing a bent molecule.

Coordinate (dative) bond: ammonium ion

A coordinate covalent bond is a covalent bond in which both shared electrons are donated by the same atom. In ammonia, nitrogen has a lone pair. It donates this pair to a proton (H⁺), which has an empty orbital.

NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺

After formation, all four N—H bonds in NH₄⁺ are equivalent. The ammonium ion has a tetrahedral arrangement around nitrogen.

Hydrogen bonding and unusual properties of water and ammonia

Hydrogen bonding is an attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (usually N, O or F) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom. It may occur between molecules (intermolecular) or within one molecule (intramolecular).

Water: Each water molecule can form an extensive hydrogen-bond network. This gives water unusually high boiling point, melting point, surface tension, specific heat and heat of vaporisation compared with similar small molecules. Ice has an open hydrogen-bonded structure, so it is less dense than liquid water and floats.

Ammonia: Hydrogen bonding between NH₃ molecules raises its boiling point compared with similar hydrides, although the hydrogen bonding is less extensive than in water. Ammonia is highly soluble in water partly because it can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.

Metallic bonding

In metals, valence electrons are delocalised throughout the solid and are not attached to one particular atom. Positive metal ion cores are held together by attraction to this mobile electron cloud, often described as an electron sea. Metallic bonding explains electrical and thermal conductivity, metallic lustre, malleability and ductility.

1.4 Solutions and Concentration Terms

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in the larger amount is generally called the solvent; the substance dissolved in it is the solute. For example, in salt water, sodium chloride is the solute and water is the solvent.

Worked example — molarity: 5.85 g NaCl is dissolved to make 500 mL solution. Molar mass of NaCl = 58.5 g mol⁻¹. Moles = 5.85/58.5 = 0.1 mol. Volume = 0.5 L. Therefore M = 0.1/0.5 = 0.20 M.

Worked example — mass percentage: 10 g sugar is dissolved in 90 g water. Mass of solution = 100 g. Mass percentage = (10/100) × 100 = 10%.

Important distinction: Molarity depends on solution volume and may change with temperature. Molality depends on mass of solvent and is essentially independent of temperature. Normality depends on the reaction because the equivalent factor can vary.

2.1 Distribution of Water on Earth

Water covers roughly 71% of Earth’s surface. However, most of it is saline ocean water. Only a small fraction is fresh water, and much of the fresh water is locked in glaciers and ice caps or stored underground. The amount readily available in rivers and lakes is very small compared with total planetary water.

Approximate proportions; not a detailed hydrological inventory.

2.2 Soft and Hard Water

Water is called soft if it readily forms lather with soap. Water is called hard if it does not readily form lather because it contains dissolved calcium and/or magnesium ions. Soap is generally the sodium or potassium salt of a higher fatty acid.

Calcium and magnesium ions react with soap to form insoluble curdy precipitates called scum. Soap is consumed in forming scum before a stable lather can develop.

2 RCOO⁻Na⁺ + Ca²⁺ → (RCOO)₂Ca ↓ + 2 Na⁺

Types of hardness and salts responsible

TypeCauseRemoval
Temporary (carbonate) hardnessBicarbonates of calcium and magnesium: Ca(HCO₃)₂, Mg(HCO₃)₂Boiling, lime treatment
Permanent (non-carbonate) hardnessChlorides, sulphates and nitrates of calcium and magnesium, e.g. CaSO₄, MgCl₂Soda-lime, zeolite or ion-exchange methods
Total hardnessTemporary + permanent hardnessDepends on treatment process

Units of hardness

Hardness is commonly expressed as mg/L or ppm as CaCO₃. For dilute water, 1 mg/L is approximately 1 ppm. Other historical units include degree Clark, degree French and milliequivalent per litre.

Hardness as CaCO₃ (mg/L) = Amount of substance (mg/L) × 50 / Equivalent weight of substance

Worked example: A water sample contains 111 mg/L of CaCl₂ (molar mass 111; equivalent weight = 111/2 = 55.5). Hardness as CaCO₃ = 111 × 50/55.5 = 100 mg/L as CaCO₃.

2.3 Problems Caused by Hard Water in Boilers

Scale and sludge formation

Scale is a hard, adherent deposit on the inner walls of boilers. It may form from precipitation of calcium carbonate, calcium sulphate, magnesium hydroxide and silica. Scale reduces heat transfer, wastes fuel, causes overheating and may lead to tube failure.

Sludge is a soft, loose, non-adherent deposit that collects in cooler or low-flow parts of a boiler. It can obstruct water circulation and pipes. Sludge is generally removed by blow-down.

Priming and foaming

Priming is the carrying of fine water droplets along with steam. It produces wet steam and may carry dissolved salts into steam lines and equipment. It is encouraged by high water level, sudden boiling and high steam demand.

Foaming is the formation of persistent bubbles or foam on the boiler-water surface, often due to oils, organic matter or high dissolved solids. Foam can promote priming. Control includes maintaining suitable water level, blow-down and appropriate treatment.

Boiler corrosion and caustic embrittlement

Dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide can cause corrosion. Oxygen attacks iron to form hydrated iron oxides. Carbon dioxide dissolves in condensate to form weak carbonic acid. Caustic embrittlement is cracking of stressed boiler steel due to local concentration of caustic alkali at stressed areas.

Prevention: Use properly softened/deionised feed water, remove dissolved gases, control pH and alkalinity, carry out blow-down, and inspect boilers regularly.

2.4 Estimation of Hardness by EDTA Method

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) forms stable 1:1 complexes with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions. In the common total-hardness titration, the sample is buffered to about pH 10 and Eriochrome Black T (EBT) indicator is added. The metal-indicator complex is wine red. As EDTA binds the metal ions, the indicator is released and the colour changes from wine red to clear blue at the end point.

  1. Take a measured volume of water sample in a conical flask.
  2. Add pH 10 ammonia-ammonium chloride buffer.
  3. Add a few drops of EBT indicator; the sample becomes wine red if hardness ions are present.
  4. Titrate with standard EDTA solution until the colour changes to blue.
  5. Use the EDTA titre and its standardisation factor to calculate hardness as CaCO₃.
Total hardness (mg/L as CaCO₃) = (VEDTA × MEDTA × 100,000) / Vsample

This form applies when EDTA molarity is used, volumes are in mL and the 1:1 complexation basis is used. In teaching laboratories, EDTA is often standardised so that 1 mL corresponds to a known mg amount of CaCO₃; then use the laboratory factor directly.

Estimation of TDS

Total dissolved solids (TDS) represent the combined concentration of dissolved inorganic salts and small amounts of organic matter in water. It is commonly measured by evaporating a known volume of filtered sample and weighing the dry residue, or estimated using a calibrated electrical-conductivity meter.

TDS (mg/L) = (Mass of dried residue in mg × 1000) / Sample volume in mL

Alkalinity of water

Alkalinity is the capacity of water to neutralise acid. It is mainly due to hydroxide (OH⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻) and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) ions. It is commonly determined by titration with standard acid using phenolphthalein and methyl orange (or a suitable mixed indicator) end points and expressed as mg/L CaCO₃.

Alkalinity as CaCO₃ (mg/L) = (mL acid × Normality of acid × 50,000) / mL sample

2.5 Water Softening Techniques

Soda-lime process

Calculated amounts of lime, Ca(OH)₂, and soda ash, Na₂CO₃, are added to hard water. Lime removes bicarbonate hardness and magnesium salts; soda ash converts soluble calcium salts into insoluble calcium carbonate. The precipitates are allowed to settle and are filtered.

Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
Mg(HCO₃)₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
CaSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄

Advantages: Economical for large quantities and reduces both temporary and permanent hardness. Limitations: Produces sludge, requires careful chemical dosing and does not usually give zero-hardness water.

Zeolite (permutit) process

Sodium zeolite is a hydrated sodium aluminosilicate. As hard water passes through a zeolite bed, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are exchanged for Na⁺ ions.

Na₂Z + Ca²⁺ → CaZ + 2Na⁺
Na₂Z + Mg²⁺ → MgZ + 2Na⁺

When exhausted, the bed is regenerated by passing concentrated sodium chloride solution through it. Calcium and magnesium are washed away as chlorides, and sodium zeolite is restored.

Ion-exchange process

Ion-exchange resins remove dissolved ions. A cation-exchange resin in hydrogen form replaces cations with H⁺; an anion-exchange resin in hydroxide form replaces anions with OH⁻. The released H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form water. This can produce demineralised water of very low ionic content.

2RH + Ca²⁺ → R₂Ca + 2H⁺
R'OH + Cl⁻ → R'Cl + OH⁻
H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

2.6 Municipal Water Treatment

  1. Screening: Removes large floating matter such as leaves, sticks and debris.
  2. Sedimentation: Water is held in tanks so heavier suspended particles settle under gravity.
  3. Coagulation and flocculation: Coagulants such as alum destabilise fine colloidal particles and help them form larger flocs that settle.
  4. Filtration: Water passes through sand and gravel or other filter media to remove remaining suspended matter and many microorganisms.
  5. Sterilisation/disinfection: Chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet treatment is used to inactivate disease-causing microorganisms. A controlled residual disinfectant may be maintained in distribution systems.
Simplified municipal treatment sequence; actual plants may use additional stages.

2.7 Drinking Water and Indian Standards

Water for drinking and cooking should be clear, palatable and free from disease-causing microorganisms and harmful chemical concentrations. A source may be a municipal supply, protected groundwater or another approved source, but it should be tested and treated as required. Boiling can inactivate many pathogens but does not remove dissolved salts, heavy metals or chemical contaminants.

3.1 Occurrence of Metals and Metallurgy

Most metals occur in nature in combined form because they react with oxygen, sulphur, carbonates and other elements. A few less reactive metals may occur in the native (free) state.

Mineral: A naturally occurring substance containing a metal or its compound. Ore: A mineral from which a metal can be extracted conveniently and economically. All ores are minerals, but all minerals are not ores.

MetalImportant oreFormula
IronHaematiteFe₂O₃
IronMagnetiteFe₃O₄
AluminiumBauxiteHydrated aluminium oxide, commonly Al₂O₃·xH₂O
CopperCopper pyrites (chalcopyrite)CuFeS₂
CopperCupriteCu₂O

Gangue (matrix): Unwanted earthy impurities such as sand, clay and rock mixed with an ore. Flux: A substance added during smelting to react with gangue. Slag: The fusible product formed by reaction of flux and gangue, which can be separated from the molten metal.

General principles of metallurgy

  1. Crushing and grinding: The ore is reduced to a suitable particle size.
  2. Concentration/ore dressing: Gangue is removed by methods such as gravity separation, magnetic separation or froth flotation.
  3. Conversion to oxide: Sulphide ores may be roasted in air; carbonate ores may be calcined in limited or no air.
  4. Reduction: Metal oxide is reduced to metal using carbon, carbon monoxide, hydrogen, aluminium or electrolysis, depending on reactivity.
  5. Refining: Impure metal is purified by electrolytic refining, distillation, liquation or other methods.

3.2 Extraction of Iron from Haematite

Iron is extracted in a blast furnace. A charge of concentrated haematite, coke and limestone is fed from the top. A hot blast of air enters near the bottom. Coke provides heat and forms carbon monoxide, the main reducing agent. Limestone decomposes to calcium oxide, which combines with silica impurities to form calcium silicate slag.

Simplified blast-furnace layout (not to scale).

Important reactions:

C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat
CO₂ + C → 2CO
Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag)

Molten iron collects near the bottom because it is denser than slag. Slag floats above it and is removed separately. The iron from a blast furnace is pig iron, which contains significant carbon and other impurities; it is further processed to make cast iron or steel.

3.3 Extraction of Aluminium from Bauxite

Bauxite is purified to obtain alumina (Al₂O₃), commonly by the Bayer process. In the Hall–Héroult process, alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆), which lowers the operating temperature and improves electrical conductivity. Electrolysis is carried out using carbon electrodes.

At cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al(l)

At anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻. The oxygen reacts with carbon anodes, producing CO₂ (and some CO), so the anodes are gradually consumed.

Overall simplified reaction: 2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂

Liquid aluminium collects at the bottom of the electrolytic cell and is tapped off. The process consumes considerable electrical energy.

3.5 Portland Cement and Hardening

Ordinary Portland cement is a hydraulic binding material made by grinding clinker with a controlled amount of gypsum. Its principal clinker compounds are tricalcium silicate (C₃S), dicalcium silicate (C₂S), tricalcium aluminate (C₃A) and tetracalcium aluminoferrite (C₄AF). These symbols are shorthand used in cement chemistry.

When water is added, cement compounds hydrate and form products that bind aggregate particles together. Calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) gel is the principal strength-giving product. Calcium hydroxide is also formed. C₃S contributes more to early strength, while C₂S contributes more to later strength. Gypsum controls the rapid reaction of C₃A and helps prevent flash setting.

Simplified: Calcium silicates + water → C-S-H gel + Ca(OH)₂

Setting is the loss of plasticity after adding water. Hardening is the gradual development of strength due to continuing hydration. Proper water curing is important because it maintains moisture for hydration and reduces shrinkage cracking.

3.6 Glass, Refractories and Composite Materials

Glass

Glass is an inorganic, non-crystalline (amorphous) material formed by cooling a melt without crystallisation. Ordinary soda-lime glass mainly contains silica (SiO₂), sodium oxide (Na₂O) and calcium oxide (CaO). Silica is the glass former; soda lowers melting temperature; lime improves chemical durability and hardness.

  • Soda-lime glass: Window panes, bottles and common containers.
  • Borosilicate glass: Contains boron oxide; has good thermal-shock and chemical resistance; laboratory glassware and cookware.
  • Lead glass: Contains lead oxide; high refractive index and density; decorative and optical uses (subject to safe-use requirements).
  • Fused silica: High-purity silica; excellent thermal-shock resistance and optical applications.

Refractory materials

Refractories are materials that retain strength and chemical stability at high temperatures. They line furnaces, kilns, reactors and boilers. Acidic refractories include silica; basic refractories include magnesite and dolomite; neutral refractories include alumina and chromite. Selection depends on operating temperature, slag chemistry, thermal shock and mechanical load.

Composite materials

A composite combines two or more distinct materials to obtain properties that are difficult to achieve with one material alone. A continuous matrix binds and protects the reinforcement, which carries much of the load. Examples include glass-fibre-reinforced plastic (GFRP), carbon-fibre composites, reinforced concrete and plywood. Applications include aircraft, automobiles, sports equipment, buildings and wind-turbine blades.

3.7 Polymers and Plastics

A polymer is a very large molecule made of repeating structural units. The small molecule used to build a polymer is a monomer. A homopolymer is made from one type of monomer; a copolymer is made from two or more different monomers. The degree of polymerisation is the average number of repeating units in a polymer chain.

Addition polymerisation: Unsaturated monomers join without elimination of a small molecule. Condensation polymerisation: Monomers containing two or more functional groups join, usually with elimination of a small molecule such as water or HCl.

Thermoplastics and thermosetting plastics

Thermoplastics soften on heating and harden on cooling repeatedly, so they can often be remoulded and recycled mechanically. Examples: PVC, PS and PTFE. Thermosetting plastics form a heavily cross-linked network during curing. Once set, they do not melt on reheating; excessive heat causes decomposition. Bakelite is a thermoset.

Rubber and vulcanisation

Natural rubber is mainly cis-1,4-polyisoprene, obtained from latex. Raw rubber is soft, sticky in warm conditions and brittle in cold conditions. In vulcanisation, rubber is heated with sulphur (and suitable additives), creating sulphur cross-links between polymer chains. This improves elasticity, tensile strength, abrasion resistance and temperature performance, while reducing stickiness and permanent deformation.

4.1 Fuels and Combustion

A fuel is a combustible substance that releases useful heat energy when it undergoes a controlled chemical reaction, usually oxidation. A good fuel should have high calorific value, moderate ignition temperature, low moisture and ash, controlled combustion, easy storage and transport, reasonable cost and low harmful emissions.

Combustion is a chemical reaction of a fuel with oxygen accompanied by heat, and often light. Complete combustion of carbon gives carbon dioxide; incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide or soot. Hydrogen burns to form water.

C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat
2C + O₂ → 2CO + heat (incomplete combustion)
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O + heat
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat

4.2 Calorific Value: HCV and LCV

Calorific value is the heat released by complete combustion of a unit quantity of fuel. It is expressed in kJ/kg for solid and liquid fuels or kJ/m³ for gases.

Higher calorific value (HCV/GCV): Includes the heat recovered when water formed during combustion is condensed. Lower calorific value (LCV/NCV): Excludes the latent heat of condensation of water vapour, because in many practical systems the water leaves as vapour.

Dulong’s formula

For a solid or liquid fuel, approximate HCV can be estimated from its ultimate analysis (mass percentages of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and sulphur).

HCV (kcal/kg) = [8080C + 34500(H − O/8) + 2240S] / 100

Here C, H, O and S are mass percentages. The formula assumes hydrogen available for combustion is reduced by the amount already combined with oxygen in the fuel.

LCV = HCV − 0.09H × 587 kcal/kg

In this commonly used form, H is the percentage of hydrogen by mass and 587 kcal/kg is the approximate latent heat of steam. In SI units, use consistent units throughout.

Example: A fuel contains C=80%, H=6%, O=8%, S=2%. H−O/8 = 6−1 = 5. HCV = [8080(80)+34500(5)+2240(2)]/100 = 82,? kcal/kg. Calculation: 646400 + 172500 + 4480 = 823380; divide by 100 = 8233.8 kcal/kg. LCV = 8233.8 − (0.09×6×587) = 7916.8 kcal/kg approximately.

4.3 Proximate Analysis of Coal

Proximate analysis determines moisture, volatile matter, ash and fixed carbon in coal. It gives a quick indication of coal quality and combustion behaviour.

  • Moisture: A known mass of coal is heated at about 105–110°C under specified conditions. Loss in mass gives moisture percentage. High moisture lowers effective heating value and increases transport cost.
  • Volatile matter: The dried coal is heated strongly in a covered crucible under standard conditions. The additional mass loss, excluding moisture, gives volatile matter. It affects ease of ignition, flame length and smoke.
  • Ash: Coal is heated in air until combustible matter burns away. The remaining inorganic residue is ash. High ash reduces useful heat and causes disposal and handling problems.
  • Fixed carbon: Calculated by difference.
Fixed carbon (%) = 100 − [Moisture (%) + Volatile matter (%) + Ash (%)]

4.4 Petrol and Diesel: Fuel Rating

Octane number measures the resistance of petrol (spark-ignition fuel) to knocking. It is defined by comparison with reference mixtures of iso-octane (assigned 100) and n-heptane (assigned 0) under specified test conditions. Higher octane fuel resists auto-ignition in the engine more effectively; it does not automatically mean greater energy content.

Cetane number indicates the ignition quality of diesel (compression-ignition fuel). A higher cetane number generally means a shorter ignition delay and easier starting/smoother combustion under suitable engine conditions. It is determined using standard reference fuels and test procedures.

4.6 Lubricants and Lubrication

A lubricant is a substance introduced between moving surfaces to reduce friction, wear and heat generation. Lubricants also help remove heat, prevent corrosion, seal clearances, reduce noise and carry away contaminants.

Characteristics of a good lubricant

  • Suitable viscosity and high viscosity index.
  • Good oiliness and film strength; low friction and wear.
  • High flash and fire points; low volatility.
  • Low pour point for cold starting and flow.
  • Good oxidation and thermal stability; low sludge and carbon-residue tendency.
  • Non-corrosive, compatible with seals and economical.

Mechanisms of lubrication

Hydrodynamic (thick-film) lubrication: A continuous fluid film completely separates moving surfaces. Relative motion draws oil into a converging wedge, generating pressure that supports the load. Metal-to-metal contact is greatly reduced. It is common in journal bearings at suitable speed and load.

Boundary (thin-film) lubrication: When a full fluid film cannot be maintained because of low speed, high load or start-stop conditions, polar molecules or additives form an adsorbed or chemically reacted film on metal surfaces. This thin layer reduces direct contact, friction and wear.

Conceptual comparison of thick-film and boundary lubrication.

Physical properties of lubricants

Viscosity: Resistance of a fluid to flow. High viscosity oils are thicker; low viscosity oils flow more easily. Viscosity must suit the speed, load and temperature of the machine.

Viscosity index (VI): An empirical measure of how much viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI means viscosity changes less as temperature changes.

Oiliness: Ability of a lubricant to adhere to surfaces and maintain a lubricating film, especially under boundary conditions.

Flash point: Lowest temperature at which vapour above the oil momentarily ignites when a test flame is applied. Fire point: Temperature at which vapour burns continuously for a specified period; it is generally higher than the flash point.

Cloud point: Temperature at which wax crystals first make an oil appear cloudy on cooling. Pour point: Lowest temperature at which the oil will still flow under specified test conditions.

Chemical properties / tests

Carbon residue (coke number): Indicates the tendency of an oil to leave carbonaceous residue after evaporation and pyrolysis under a standard test. Excessive residue can foul engines and equipment.

Total acid number (TAN): Milligrams of potassium hydroxide required to neutralise acidic constituents in one gram of oil. Rising TAN can indicate oxidation or contamination.

Saponification value: Milligrams of KOH required to saponify one gram of oil or fat. It is useful for characterising oils containing saponifiable esters; mineral oils generally have very low values.

5.1 Oxidation, Reduction and Redox Reactions

In the electronic concept, oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain of electrons. They always occur together in a redox reaction because electrons released by one species are accepted by another.

Oxidation: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
Reduction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Overall: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

The substance that loses electrons is the reducing agent; it causes reduction of another species. The substance that gains electrons is the oxidising agent; it causes oxidation of another species.

5.2 Electrolytes and Non-electrolytes

Electrolytes are substances that conduct electricity in molten state or aqueous solution because they produce mobile ions. Examples include NaCl, HCl, H₂SO₄ and NaOH in suitable states or solutions. Non-electrolytes do not produce significant ions in solution and therefore do not conduct appreciably; examples include sugar and ethanol in water.

Strong electrolytes ionise or dissociate almost completely; weak electrolytes ionise only partially. The strength of an electrolyte is not the same as the concentration of its solution.

5.3 Electrolysis and Faraday’s Laws

Electrolysis is the chemical change produced when direct electric current passes through an electrolyte. The apparatus contains two electrodes dipped in electrolyte and connected to a DC supply. Cations move toward the cathode (negative electrode in an electrolytic cell) and gain electrons; anions move toward the anode (positive electrode) and lose electrons.

Faraday’s first law: The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

m ∝ Q;   Q = It;   m = ZIt = (MIt)/(nF)

Faraday’s second law: When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses deposited are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights.

m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂    where E = molar mass / number of electrons transferred

F is Faraday’s constant, approximately 96,485 C mol⁻¹; I is current in ampere; t is time in seconds; M is molar mass; n is electrons per ion; Z is electrochemical equivalent.

Numerical example: A current of 2 A is passed through a CuSO₄ solution for 30 minutes. For Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, M=63.5 g/mol and n=2. Q = 2 × 30 × 60 = 3600 C. Mass = (63.5 × 3600)/(2 × 96485) = 1.18 g approximately.

5.4 Industrial Applications of Electrolysis

Electrometallurgy

Electrometallurgy uses electrical energy to extract or produce metals. Highly reactive metals such as aluminium, sodium and magnesium are commonly obtained by electrolysis of molten compounds because chemical reducing agents are not suitable or economical. The Hall–Héroult process for aluminium is an important example.

Electroplating

Electroplating deposits a thin layer of one metal on another object by electrolysis. The object to be coated is generally made the cathode; the coating metal may be the anode; and the electrolyte contains ions of the coating metal. It improves appearance, corrosion resistance, wear resistance and sometimes electrical properties.

Example: In copper plating, Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons at the cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The anode may dissolve: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻. The article must be cleaned thoroughly before plating to obtain a uniform, adherent coating.

Electrolytic refining

In electrolytic refining, impure metal is made the anode, a thin sheet of pure metal is the cathode and a suitable soluble salt of the metal is the electrolyte. Metal dissolves from the anode and deposits in pure form on the cathode. Insoluble impurities may collect below the anode as anode mud; soluble impurities remain in solution or are managed through electrolyte purification. Copper refining is a common example.

Simplified electrolytic refining arrangement.

5.5 Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode in both galvanic and electrolytic cells. In a galvanic cell, the anode is negative and the cathode positive; in an electrolytic cell, the anode is positive and the cathode negative.

Primary cell — dry cell

A common zinc-carbon dry cell has a zinc container as the anode (negative terminal), a carbon rod surrounded by a mixture containing manganese dioxide as the cathode depolariser, and a moist electrolyte paste containing ammonium chloride and/or zinc chloride. During discharge, zinc is oxidised and the cathode mixture participates in reduction reactions. The cell is inexpensive and convenient but is not designed for normal recharging.

Secondary cell — lead storage battery

A lead-acid battery is rechargeable. Its negative plate is spongy lead (Pb), positive plate is lead dioxide (PbO₂), and electrolyte is dilute sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄). During discharge, both plates are converted toward lead sulphate and water is formed; the acid concentration and specific gravity decrease. Charging reverses the reaction.

Overall discharge reaction: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

A single lead-acid cell has a nominal voltage of about 2 V. Six cells connected in series make a nominal 12 V battery. Uses include automobiles, inverters, emergency lighting and backup power systems. Proper ventilation, correct charging and protection from acid are important.

Fuel cells

A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of a continuously supplied fuel and oxidant directly into electrical energy. In a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen is supplied to the anode and oxygen to the cathode. The overall reaction is:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O + electrical energy + heat

Fuel cells can be efficient and have low local pollutant emissions when hydrogen is used, but cost, hydrogen production, storage, distribution and durability are important practical considerations.

Solar cells

A solar cell is a semiconductor device that converts light energy directly into electrical energy by the photovoltaic effect. A common cell uses a p-n junction in silicon. Photons create electron-hole pairs; the junction’s internal electric field separates charges and produces a potential difference. Solar cells are connected into modules and arrays for larger power output. They are used in calculators, rooftop systems, street lighting, satellites and solar power plants.

5.6 Corrosion of Metals

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal due to chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment. Rusting of iron is a common example. Corrosion causes loss of material, reduced strength, leakage, equipment failure and economic loss.

Types of corrosion

Dry (chemical) corrosion: Direct chemical reaction of a metal with dry gases, usually at elevated temperature, forming oxide, sulphide or other surface films. The rate depends on temperature, gas nature and the protectiveness of the film.

Wet (electrochemical) corrosion: Occurs when small anodic and cathodic regions develop on a metal surface in the presence of an electrolyte such as moisture. Metal dissolves at the anode while a reduction reaction occurs at the cathode.

Hydrogen liberation mechanism

This mechanism commonly occurs in acidic solutions. At anodic areas, iron dissolves; at cathodic areas, hydrogen ions gain electrons and form hydrogen gas.

Anode: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂↑
Overall: Fe + 2H⁺ → Fe²⁺ + H₂↑

Oxygen absorption mechanism

This mechanism commonly occurs in neutral or alkaline water containing dissolved oxygen. Iron dissolves at the anodic area. At the cathodic area, oxygen is reduced in the presence of water to form hydroxide ions. Ferrous ions react with hydroxide and oxygen to form hydrated iron oxides (rust).

Anode: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂; further oxidation forms hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).
Different regions of a wet metal surface can act as anode and cathode.

Factors affecting corrosion rate

  • Nature and purity of the metal; composition and microstructure.
  • Position in the galvanic series and contact with dissimilar metals.
  • Moisture, oxygen, pH, temperature and dissolved salts.
  • Electrical conductivity and movement of the electrolyte.
  • Stress, surface condition, deposits and differential aeration.
  • Nature of the corrosion-product film: protective films slow corrosion; porous or non-adherent films do not.

5.7 Corrosion Prevention

Internal methods

Purification: Removing impurities and inclusions reduces local galvanic cells and non-uniform corrosion. Alloying: Adding elements such as chromium and nickel can improve corrosion resistance; stainless steel forms a thin, adherent chromium-rich passive film. Heat treatment: Suitable heat treatment can reduce residual stresses, improve structural uniformity and reduce susceptibility to certain forms of corrosion; the correct treatment depends on the alloy and service condition.

External methods — metallic coatings

Anodic (sacrificial) coating: The coating metal is more active than the base metal. Zinc coating on iron (galvanising) is a common example. Even if scratched, zinc can corrode preferentially and protect exposed iron in many environments.

Cathodic (noble) coating: The coating metal is more noble than the base metal, such as tin on steel. It acts as a barrier. If the coating is damaged, exposed steel may corrode rapidly because it becomes the anode coupled to the larger noble coating area. Therefore, the coating must remain intact.

Organic coatings and inhibitors

Paints, varnishes, enamels, polymers and protective oils form barriers that keep moisture and oxygen away from the metal. Surface preparation and correct application are essential. Corrosion inhibitors are chemicals added in small amounts to an environment to reduce corrosion by slowing anodic dissolution, cathodic reactions or both. They are selected for the metal, fluid and operating conditions; they should be handled according to safety instructions.

Practical selection: Choose protection according to environment, operating temperature, expected service life, mechanical damage, maintenance access and cost. A coating is only reliable when the surface is correctly cleaned and the coating is inspected and maintained.
